01 JULY 2025 · 6 MIN READ · #REINFORCEMENT-LEARNING #DQN #LESSONS Building a self-driving DQN agent from scratch Designing the environment, the reward function, and the training loop myself — and what three failed reward designs taught me about reinforcement learning. Read →

This project began with more confidence than experience. I had just finished the DeepLearning.AI machine learning courses and wanted to apply reinforcement learning end-to-end — not through a pre-built Gymnasium environment, but by designing the environment, the reward function, and the training loop myself.

THE SETUP

The project was a self-driving car simulation: a top-down Pygame environment, a car with eight LiDAR rays whose distance readings form the state, and a Deep Q-Network agent choosing between steering, accelerating, and braking. The simulation itself came together in a week — the road, the obstacles, and the sensors all worked as intended. The difficult part came next.

REWARD DESIGN — THE HARD PART

What no course conveys clearly enough is that the reward function is harder to get right than the neural network.

My first design — +1 for staying in lane, −10 for a collision — produced an agent that parked in the middle of the lane and rotated slowly: technically reward-maximising, practically useless. The second, rewarding speed, produced an agent that drove flat out in a straight line and ignored every corner.

The third design finally worked. It had four components: a proportional penalty for lane deviation, a reward for smooth forward velocity rather than maximum velocity, a heavy collision penalty, and a small penalty for abrupt steering changes. That last term proved decisive — without it the agent twitched constantly between left and right; with it, it learned to prefer smooth trajectories. Reaching this design took three weeks of watching the agent optimise for the wrong objective in increasingly inventive ways.

THE HYPERPARAMETER GRIND

DQNs are notoriously sensitive: learning rate, epsilon decay, replay buffer size, and target network update frequency all interact, and there is no formula for setting them. The only workable approach was disciplined experimentation — change one variable, observe the result, record it. I kept a spreadsheet of every run; the 50 commits over four months are mostly entries along the lines of "lr=0.0005, slower epsilon decay."

WHAT I TOOK AWAY

I expected to learn about network architectures. What I actually learned was different. Reward design is the core skill in reinforcement learning: the network learns exactly what you incentivise, so strange behaviour almost always traces back to the reward. Iteration speed matters more than architectural sophistication — running more experiments faster beats days spent designing the perfect network. And logging everything is non-negotiable: records of the agent's behaviour across conditions were the only way I caught bugs.

The repository is honestly labelled a proof of concept — the agent navigates its simulation and nothing more. But building every component myself, and making every possible mistake along the way, taught me reinforcement learning at a depth no tutorial could have.

02 JUNE 2026 · 5 MIN READ · #DISTRIBUTED-SYSTEMS #KAFKA #ENGINEERING How I think about distributed systems as a final-year student What operating 50,000 events per second actually involves — and why the hardest bugs live in the interactions between components, not the components themselves. Read →

I spent two months at Jio Platforms working on Kafka-based data pipelines. Before that, I had built a distributed AI inference platform as a personal project. Between the two, I have formed some early opinions about distributed systems — not expert opinions, but the kind that only come from watching systems fail in ways a textbook never covers.

WHAT UNIVERSITY TEACHES

Coursework frames distributed systems through the CAP theorem, consensus algorithms, and replication strategies — the theory. None of it is wrong, but it is incomplete in one specific way: it does not convey what operating 50,000 events per second actually involves.

WHAT PRODUCTION TAUGHT ME

The pipelines I worked on at Jio processed over 50,000 events per second across five topics. In practice, that means every configuration decision has consequences that surface immediately in monitoring.

Consumer group tuning is a good example. In theory, adding consumers to a group increases throughput. In practice, it changes how partitions are assigned, which triggers rebalances, which pause processing. We reduced replication lag by 30% not by adding resources but by tuning how the existing consumers were configured — an operational insight, not an architectural one.

The Prometheus and Grafana dashboards were equally essential. Without them I had no way to tell whether a change helped or hurt. Observability is not a nice-to-have; it is how you know what your system is actually doing.

WHERE DISTRIBUTED BUGS LIVE

Bugs in distributed systems are rarely confined to a single component; they live in the interactions between components.

In my inference platform, requests were occasionally processed twice. The fault was not in the API gateway or the workers, but in how the Kafka consumer acknowledged messages: if a worker crashed after processing a message but before committing its offset, Kafka redelivered the message to another worker. Finding that bug required reasoning about three components simultaneously — which is precisely what makes these systems difficult.

A USEFUL MENTAL MODEL

I now think of a distributed system as a set of contracts: each service promises to accept requests in a given format, process them, and respond in a defined way. The interesting engineering questions follow directly. What happens when a service cannot keep its contract? Circuit breakers. When a message is delivered twice? Idempotency. When two services hold inconsistent views of the same data? Eventual consistency. These are not exam questions — they are certainties in any system that runs long enough.

WHAT I WOULD TELL MYSELF A YEAR AGO

Build something that genuinely breaks: real concurrency, real message queues, real failure modes. The textbook teaches the vocabulary; only building teaches the language. My inference platform failed constantly in its early stages, and every failure taught me something the theory had not — the internship at Jio confirmed most of it.

I am still a student, but I understand these systems differently now, and that difference came entirely from building things and watching them fail.
